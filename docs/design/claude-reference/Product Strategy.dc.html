01

Product strategy

What this product is, and what it refuses to be

Most astrology apps tell you what the sky is doing. This one tells you what to do about it — and shows its work.

The category splits into two failures. Mass-market apps generate pleasant, unfalsifiable paragraphs that could apply to anyone — no reasoning, no accountability, no reason to return beyond habit. Serious tools dump ephemeris data on people who can't read it. The gap is a product that is legible at a glance and inspectable underneath: every claim it makes can be unfolded down to the transit that produced it.

That single idea — evidence on demand — is the spine of the whole design. It drives the three-layer disclosure model, the "Why the stars suggest this" block, the Compare Systems feature, and even Roast Mode, which is allowed to change the voice but never the evidence.

Pillar I

Daily, and reasoned

A morning read that names the influence, translates it into a decision, and offers the astrological receipt. The retention engine.

Pillar II

Relationship intelligence

Not a compatibility calculator. A standing model of the people in your life, with core dynamics and daily conditions held visibly apart.

Pillar III

Two voices, one mind

Normal and Roast are the same intelligence with different manners. The distribution engine — and the reason people screenshot it.

Designed for

The regular daily reader — checks in most mornings, casual fluency, knows their Big Three, can't read an aspect grid. Every default is tuned to her: balanced reading length, plain-language first layer, terminology always glossed on first use. The newcomer is carried by the same first layer; the practitioner is satisfied by layer three.

The three-second test

Every screen answers one question in three seconds, in one sentence of editorial type. If a screen needs a second sentence to be understood, it is two screens — or the wrong hierarchy. This is the review criterion I'll hold each screen to.

02

Product personality

A well-read friend who happens to know astronomy

Not an oracle, not a therapist, not a chatbot. The product speaks like someone who has read your chart carefully, likes you, and isn't going to flatter you. It states tendencies, never fates. It says "may" and "tends to" and "if" — and it never claims to know what will happen.

Always
  • Specific before poetic — name the placement, then the feeling
  • Conditional, not predictive — "tends to", never "will"
  • Actionable — every reading ends in something you could do
  • Accountable — the evidence is always one tap away
  • Adult — it assumes you can handle a real observation
Never
  • Mystical filler — "the universe is aligning for you"
  • Fear as engagement — no ominous warnings to drive opens
  • Diagnosis — no psychological or medical claims, in either mode
  • Verdicts on people — no "incompatible", no "toxic"
  • Emoji, exclamation marks, or hype in Normal Mode
Normal voice

Your need for independence may make emotional conversations harder today.

Observant, calm, useful. Reads as though written for you specifically.

Roast voice

You want emotional intimacy, but replying four hours later is apparently part of the strategy.

Same observation, same evidence. Sharper delivery. Level 3 of 5 — it lands, it doesn't wound.

03

Information architecture

Four places, three cross-cutting systems

Three things are not destinations, and treating them as destinations is the mistake most astrology apps make. Mode (Normal/Roast), System (Western/Vedic/Chinese) and Ask are global layers that reach into every screen. Give them tabs and they become chores; make them ambient and they become the product.

Why "Ask" isn't a tab

A tab labelled Ask produces a blank chat box, which produces generic questions, which produces generic answers — a chatbot wearing an astrology skin. Attached to context, the same feature becomes "ask about this": a button under a friction dimension already knows the person, the aspect and today's transits. The question is half-written before the user types.

Why Cosmic and Calendar merged

They are the same axis at different distances: what the sky is doing now and what it will do next. As separate tabs both feel thin and users can't tell which to open. As Sky → Now / Ahead, one segmented control spans from this hour to next season, and the calendar inherits the traffic that cosmic weather generates.

Circle > Compatibility

A calculator is visited once per person. A circle is visited daily, because the people in it have a today. Renaming the tab changes the mental model from "run a comparison" to "check on us" — and that is the retention difference between a feature and a habit.

Sky = Now + Ahead

One segmented control at the top of Sky spans this hour to next season. Cosmic weather earns the visit; the calendar catches the curiosity it creates. Neither can carry a tab alone.

No fifth tab, ever

Ask, Mode, System, Settings and Share all stay off the bar. Four one-word destinations is the ceiling; anything that wants to be fifth is really a layer, and layers belong in context.

iOS

Bottom tab bar with hairline top rule, large-title collapse on scroll, sheet presentation for evidence layers, swipe-back everywhere, Dynamic Type honoured to XXL, haptics on the mode index.

Android — the deltas

Same four destinations in a Material navigation bar (no icon pills — a hairline indicator instead), top app bar rather than large title, bottom sheets with a drag handle, predictive back, and edge-to-edge with the system bars tinted to the surface. One brand, two grammars.

07

Brand principles

Six rules that decide arguments

01

Show the work

Every claim can be unfolded to the transit behind it. Nothing is asserted that the product can't source.

02

The page is the interface

Structure comes from rules, type size and surface — not from floating cards. If a thing is in a box, ask what the box is for.

03

Color is stroke and state

Gilt draws lines, marks and underlines. It never fills a shape larger than a chip. Hue always carries meaning, never mood.

04

Light and shadow mean something

Ink is for the sky and for consequence. Paper is for reading. A surface change is a change of register, never a decoration.

05

Numbers are typography

Degrees, dates, times and indices are set as figures — tabular, aligned, sized to their importance. They carry composition, not chrome.

06

Restraint is the brand

One accent, one display face, one idea per screen. Everything the product declines to do is part of why it feels expensive.

Rules that keep it from sloping into pastiche
  • The serif never sets a paragraph over three lines, a button, a form label, or anything a user must scan quickly.
  • Italic is reserved for the emotional half of a headline — one clause per screen, never a whole sentence.
  • Mono is never used for prose. If it's a sentence, it isn't mono.
  • All figures are tabular. Times, degrees and scores align in a column or they're wrong.
  • Dynamic Type: body scales to 200%; display caps at 1.4× and re-wraps; mono caps at 1.2× and falls back to Plex Sans above 1.3× so tables don't shatter.

11

Data visualization language

One idiom, applied everywhere: marks on a shared axis

No progress rings, no donuts, no dashboard gauges. Every chart in the product is the same grammar — a line that means something, marks placed on it, and the distance between marks as the message. Learn one chart, read them all. Position and shape carry the data; hue only reinforces.

Primitive 01

The Horizon — daily energy

The day as a horizon line, sunrise to sunrise. Above the line is available energy, below it is drag; transits sit as ticks with their exact times. Used on Today, in Today Together, and inside category detail.

Primitive 02

The Weave — two people, one axis

Each relationship dimension is one line; both people are placed on it. The gap is the insight. A short connector reads as alignment, a long one as difference — and the connector's style says whether that difference is complementary (dashed gilt) or abrasive (solid cinnabar). No percentages required.

Primitive 03

The Tracks — Compare Systems

Three tracks, one topic axis. Where marks align vertically the systems agree and a consensus band lights behind them; where one drifts, it's labelled as divergence — not as an error. This is what turns system choice from a filter into a feature.

Primitive 04

The Ledger — balance & calendar

Counted things become a segmented hairline bar with tabular figures; time becomes a tick ledger where height is significance and only genuinely meaningful days get a glyph. The calendar stays mostly empty on purpose.

Every chart ships an alternative. Each visualization has a screen-reader summary written as a sentence ("Communication: you and Sara sit far apart; the difference tends to be abrasive rather than complementary") and a "read as text" toggle that swaps the graphic for a table. A chart nobody can read is decoration.

12

Motion

Four verbs. Nothing bounces, nothing loops, nothing sparkles.

RISE · 420ms

Things arrive from below

Content enters with a 16px lift and fade, staggered 60ms, easing (.2,.7,.2,1). The Big Three reveal is this verb at its slowest — one placement at a time.

DRAW · 900ms

Lines inscribe themselves

Charts stroke on once — wheel, horizon, weave. Never on re-render, never in a loop. It reads as an instrument taking a reading.

STRIKE · 180ms

Depth lands, it doesn't slide

Opening an evidence layer sets it in place — 1.015 → 1.0 with the rule above it drawing outward from the tap point. Letterpress, not drawer.

TURN · 320ms

The index rotates

Mode switching, person switching and system switching all use one gesture-and-rotation. Switching is a physical act in this product.

Where motion is allowed to be felt
  • Calculating your chart — the wheel draws itself while real placements are named in mono, one line at a time. The wait becomes the value.
  • The reveal — Sun, Moon, Rising rise in sequence, 900ms apart, each holding alone before the next.
  • Compatibility weaving — the two people's marks travel to their positions and the connectors draw between them.
  • Mode turn — the whole screen's type re-strikes in place; the accent index rotates 180°; one haptic tick.
  • Full Moon / eclipse days — Today's ink header holds a slowly drifting shadow terminator. Once per event, never ambient.
Reduced Motion — a real design, not a disable

Rise becomes a 120ms opacity fade. Draw renders complete. Strike becomes an instant state change with the rule already present. Turn keeps its haptic and its color change but drops the rotation. The reveal keeps its pacing — placements still appear one at a time, because the sequencing is the emotional content, not the animation.

Nothing in the product ever animates on a loop, and nothing moves that the user didn't cause — except the two ceremonial moments above.

14

Normal & Roast

One intelligence, two manners — and a dial you can feel

The governing rule: Roast changes the voice and the accent. It never changes the evidence.

Stratum 3 — the transit, the orb, the timing — is byte-identical in both modes. That single constraint is what keeps Roast from becoming a novelty skin: the joke is always about something true, and a user who laughs can immediately tap through to why it's true. It also means the tone can be turned up without turning the product into a toy.

The Index — the switch

Press and hold the index in any header. An arc opens under your thumb and the headline rewrites live as you drag across it — you read the roast before you commit to it. Release to set. One haptic tick at the crossing.

A tap toggles outright for people who already know. Onboarding asks once, in the user's own words — "How should we talk to you?" — with both voices shown writing the same insight, so the choice is made by reading, not by label.

Level 3 of 5 — where you set it

Sharp enough to be worth screenshotting, kind enough to open every morning. The target is always a pattern — the four-hour reply, the third rewritten text, the "I'm fine" — never a person's worth.

Hard limits, enforced in copy review

15

Compatibility concept

A standing model of a relationship, plus today's weather over it

The whole feature turns on one distinction the user must grasp without being told: what you two are versus what today is doing to you. I'm giving that distinction a permanent visual grammar rather than a label.

Core dynamics — permanent

Drawn from the two natal charts. Solid, ink-weight, anchored. This is the relationship's constitution — it reads the same on any day you open it, which is exactly why it can be trusted.

Today's influence — temporary

Today's transits pull one or both of you off your usual position — shown as a dashed drift arrow to a hollow ghost mark. The core marks stay visible underneath, so "today is unusual for us" is legible at a glance, not asserted in a sentence.

The report, in the order it's read
01  The verdict, in one sentence
"Easy to talk to, hard to argue with." — set large in serif. The orientation score sits beside it in small tabular figures, deliberately outweighed.
02  The Weave — all thirteen dimensions on one screen, sorted by gap. You see where you connect and where you differ before reading a word.
03  Why you connect — the two or three placements doing the real work.
04  Where it goes wrong — named as a pattern, with the situation that triggers it.
05  What each needs — the only two-column layout in the product. Symmetry is the point.
06  What would help — two or three concrete, non-obvious moves.
Language adapts to the relationship
SAME ASPECT · ☾ ♂ SQUARE
Partner → "Heat that turns into either passion or a slammed door, depending on the hour."
Coworker → "You push each other's pace. Useful on deadlines, costly in meetings."
Parent → "Old friction with a familiar shape — it rarely resolves, but it can be scheduled around."

Relationship type is asked first, before birth data, because it governs every string, which dimensions appear at all (Attraction and Romance are simply absent for family and coworkers), and what "improve this" can responsibly mean.

Two phrases are banned outright: incompatible and any percentage presented as a verdict. A number may orient; only the interpretation may conclude.

Ortus
RECOMMENDED · OR-tus · 2 syl

Latin for rising — which is literally what an Ascendant is, and figuratively what the product does every morning. Five letters, no cluster a non-English speaker stumbles on, no astrology cliché, and it hands the design system its central motion verb (rise) and its central image (a hard horizon with something coming up over it). The wordmark sets beautifully in Cormorant at wide tracking. Downside: the meaning needs one sentence of explanation — which is fine, because that sentence is the app's opening line.

Orbis
OR-bis · 2 syl

The orb is the tolerance around an aspect — how close two planets must be for the connection to count. A quietly perfect name for a relationship product: it's a word about proximity and margin. Practitioners get the joke instantly; nobody else is excluded. Risk: sits close to "orbit", so it's the least surprising of the five.

Vesper
VES-per · 2 syl

The evening star — Venus at dusk. The warmest and most romantic of the set, which suits the compatibility pillar and suits Roast Mode's evening tone. Travels well, reads elegant rather than mystical. Risk: it leans evening, and the product's core habit is a morning one.

Parallax
PA-ra-lax · 3 syl

The apparent shift in a star's position depending on where you stand — the exact idea behind Compare Systems, and behind two people describing the same argument differently. The most intellectually satisfying name here and the strongest concept. Risk: three syllables, an X, and a slightly technical mouth-feel; the hardest to say aloud in non-English markets.

Cusp
KUSP · 1 syl

The boundary between two houses or signs — and, in ordinary English, being on the verge of something. One syllable, four letters, extremely app-store-able, and the only name here that carries a feeling and a technical meaning at once. Risk: the double meaning only lands in English, and the single closed syllable is harder to make elegant as a wordmark.

Used Ortus as the placeholder in the mockups above so you can judge it in situ. All five are pre-checked for the obvious traps — no purple-mystic connotation, no existing astrology app collision, no unfortunate meaning in the major European or Latin American markets — but none has had a trademark search, which should happen before any of this reaches production.

Positions taken early

States, privacy and accessibility decisions that shape screens rather than follow them

Missing data is a design, not an error

No birth time is the most common real state, so it gets a real design: the product runs a solar houses model, marks every affected reading with a small hollow glyph in the coordinate rail, and says plainly what is and isn't reliable. No nagging, no invented noon.

Approximate time shows Rising as a range across two signs rather than a guess — an honest answer that's also more interesting than a wrong one.

Privacy as a reason, not a warning

Every request for sensitive data is answered at the point of asking, in one line, in plain type: birth time sets your Rising sign and your houses — the difference between a horoscope and a reading. Optional fields say "optional" in the label, not in a footnote.

Saved people get an explicit line: their data is used only to compare with yours, is never shared, and deleting the person deletes it. One tap, no confirmation maze — and a "what's stored about Sara" view that's readable, not a legal document.

Accessibility built into the primitives

Because meaning lives in position, shape and glyph, the color-blind and high-contrast paths need almost no separate work. Every chart carries a sentence summary and a text alternative. Every astrological term is glossed on first appearance in a session.

The two contrast risks are known and handled: gilt-on-paper never sets body text (it steps to #8A6524), and ivory-on-ink long-form is capped — anything over ~120 words moves to paper.
